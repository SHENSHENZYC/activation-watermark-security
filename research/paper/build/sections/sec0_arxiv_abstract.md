# arXiv metadata abstract (plain text, at most 1,920 characters; not part of the PDF)

Activation-steering watermarks add a secret vector to a hidden layer during generation and detect it from the model's activations when the text is re-read; their authors motivate the design by robustness to paraphrase. We present, to our knowledge, the first security analysis of such a watermark, the construction of LLM Self-Recognition, on Qwen2.5-1.5B at 256 tokens under 6 pre-registered protocols against a passive attacker who holds the open weights and up to 1,024 watermarked outputs but cannot query the detector. First, the published strength did not reproduce on 1-3B base models: no strength made the scheme's trained probe reliably detectable without a perplexity cost. Second, that probe accepts steering that is not the key, so an attacker without the key produces fluent forgeries it accepts at the one strength where quality is acceptable. Third, an exact key-resampling score test, a transfer of GaussMark's statistic, attributes 96.0-100.0% of genuine texts at a 1% false-positive rate that is exact on average over keys, and rejects those forgeries. Fourth, the same statistic averaged over 64 outputs recovers the fixed key at the known layer (median cosine 0.940 and 0.951), finds the layer for most keys by search, and yields forgeries attributed as often as genuine text. Fifth, a paraphrase scrubs the test's evidence at the quality-acceptable strengths and not above them. Sixth, keying the direction by the previous h tokens or rotating among 8 keys, transfers from token-level watermarking, blocks this attacker at n <= 1,024; context keying improves perplexity but loses most of the paraphrase robustness, and rotation falls to a clustering attacker from 64 texts. For the fixed key on this model, the detectable and paraphrase-robust strengths carry a quality cost, the quality-neutral strengths are scrubbed, and the published detector type is forgeable at the quality-neutral strength.
